Featured Artifact 01

AI Lab: Career Prep Assistant

AIML-500 · AI Lab Guided Activity

Career Prep Assistant — An AI Coaching Chatbot Built Through Design Thinking

A conversational AI assistant that turns a job seeker's real experience into tailored, honest STAR-format interview answers and resume bullet points, built end-to-end using the design thinking process.

Description

Career Prep Assistant is a working chatbot that helps technical professionals translate real accomplishments into interview-ready language. Rather than generating generic advice, it asks clarifying questions when context is thin, then produces structured, STAR-labeled answers or quantified resume bullets grounded in what the user actually tells it.

Objective

I built this after noticing, in my own job search, that strong technical work often gets undersold in interviews and on resumes simply because translating it into that format is a different skill than doing the work itself. The goal was an assistant that closes that gap without ever inflating what's true.

Process
  1. Empathy: identified that technical candidates often struggle to reframe accomplishments for interviews and resumes, and that any tool touching this space risks tempting users toward exaggeration.
  2. Define: scoped the problem narrowly to interview and resume language, not job matching or applications.
  3. Ideate: compared a static bullet generator, a mock interview simulator, and a conversational coach, and chose the conversational format for its flexibility.
  4. Prototype: built and tested a system prompt with explicit guardrails, then deployed it on Chatbase for public, login-free access.
  5. Test: validated it against a real interview-prep scenario, a resume-bullet scenario, and a deliberate attempt to get it to fabricate a credential.
Tools and Technologies Used
ChatbasePrompt Engineering Anthropic Claude API (prototype)Design Thinking
Value Proposition
For hiring managers evaluating GenAI engineering ability: this artifact shows I can take an AI product from a real user problem through working guardrails to a publicly deployed tool, not just describe how one would work.
Unique Value

The guardrail design is the differentiator. Most quick chatbot demos optimize for helpfulness alone. This one is explicitly instructed to refuse fabricated credentials and redirect to honest alternatives, tested live with an adversarial prompt, which reflects a responsible-AI mindset rather than just a working demo.

Relevance

This directly supports the GenAI and hybrid Java/AI engineering roles I'm targeting, where the ability to design safe, well-scoped AI features matters as much as raw model access.

Featured Artifact 02

AI, Hot and Cold: A History, 1950–2026

AIML-500 · AI/ML History Timeline

A Scroll-Driven Timeline of AI Winters and Booms

A researched, single-page timeline tracing AI/ML history from 1950 to today through the lens of its funding and confidence cycles, built as a scrollytelling web page rather than a static list of dates.

Description

Instead of a flat chronological list, this artifact frames AI/ML history around a pattern that a plain list of dates would hide: the field repeatedly surges on hype, stalls when results fall short of the promises, then breaks through again once a new idea or enough compute arrives. A confidence line runs down the page as the reader scrolls, rising through breakthrough eras and dropping through the AI winters, so the shape of the field's history is visible, not just its dates.

Objective

I wanted a resource that explained why AI progress looks the way it does, not just what happened when. Framing the timeline around funding and confidence cycles, rather than a simple list of milestones, forces the events to connect to each other instead of standing alone.

Process
  1. Research: gathered milestone dates and causes from academic and industry sources, including a peer-reviewed history of the Lighthill report and multiple explainers on AI winters, then cross-checked events across sources before including them.
  2. Framing: chose the winters-and-booms narrative over a plain chronological list, since it better explains the field's pace to a reader with no AI background.
  3. Build: built the page in plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, using scroll position to animate a funding/confidence indicator alongside each era.
  4. Editorial pass: wrote and rewrote every description in my own words, selected which milestones made the cut, and made the final design and framing decisions myself.
  5. Disclosure: documented exactly where and how I used AI assistance (research support, fact-checking, first-draft code) directly on the page, rather than leaving it unstated.

Value Proposition
For hiring managers evaluating research and front-end craft: this artifact shows I can take primary-source research, distill it into a clear editorial argument, and ship it as a polished, interactive web experience, not just static text.
Unique Value

This is a different skill set than the Career Prep Assistant chatbot: less about conversational design and guardrails, more about research synthesis, information design, and front-end implementation. The explicit AI-disclosure section is also a deliberate choice, showing how I used AI as a research and drafting aid while keeping editorial control and final judgment my own.

Relevance

Machine Learning Training Methods Coach

AIML-500 · AI Lab Guided Activity

Building Conceptual Mastery Through AI-Guided Dialogue

A recorded, real-time conversation with an AI tutor working through the seven foundational questions of machine learning training: how supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning models learn, why algorithms and repetition matter, and what role data quality plays in generalization and bias.

Description

Rather than reading a static explanation of ML training methods, I worked through the core concepts in a live back-and-forth with an AI coach, attempting my own answer to each question before receiving feedback, then pushing further with follow-up questions rather than accepting the first explanation. The conversation moved from supervised learning's labeled-data feedback loop, through unsupervised clustering, into reinforcement learning's reward-driven trial and error, and closed on how data volume and quality determine whether a model generalizes or overfits.

Objective

I wanted to test whether I actually understood these training paradigms well enough to reason through them myself, not just recognize the right answer when I saw it. Attempting each answer first, before the AI confirmed or corrected it, was a deliberate choice to surface any gaps in my understanding rather than let a fluent explanation paper over them.

Process
  1. Attempt first: for each of the seven required questions, I drafted my own answer before the AI responded, using concepts from earlier in the conversation to build toward later ones.
  2. Extend, don't just confirm: when the AI validated an answer, I followed up with a harder question rather than moving on immediately, for example asking whether too much repetition could hurt a model (leading into overfitting) and how practitioners catch biased data before it reaches a trained model.
  3. Connect across the conversation: later answers, like the training loop and the role of data, explicitly tied back to concepts established earlier (weight updates, algorithms, epochs), rather than treating each of the seven questions as isolated.
  4. Close on responsible AI: the conversation ended on a question about detecting bias in training data, connecting this technical foundation directly to the fairness and governance work I've done elsewhere in this program.

Featured Artifact 04

AI-Guided Data Challenge Scenarios

AIML-500 · AI Lab Guided Activity

Reasoning Through Real-World ML Data Problems in Live Dialogue

A recorded, real-time conversation with an AI coach working through three progressively harder data challenges in machine learning: handling missing data, correcting severe class imbalance, and identifying and mitigating algorithmic bias in a loan approval model.

Description

Rather than describing these challenges in the abstract, I worked through three concrete scenarios in a live back-and-forth with an AI coach, proposing my own approach to each before receiving feedback, then pushing further with clarifying and follow-up questions instead of accepting the first answer at face value. The conversation moved from missing-data strategy, through class-imbalance techniques and evaluation metrics, into the more ambiguous territory of algorithmic fairness and proxy discrimination.

Objective

I wanted to test whether I could reason through realistic, messy data problems the way I would on the job, not just recall the name of a technique. Each scenario intentionally had more than one defensible answer, so the goal was to weigh tradeoffs out loud and justify a specific choice, rather than pattern-match to a textbook response.

Process
  1. Scenario 1, missing data: given a churn dataset with 30% missing values across two fields, I separated the columns by likely cause of missingness rather than applying one blanket fix, median-imputing a field I judged to be randomly missing, and adding a missing-value flag for a field where absence itself looked like a churn signal.
  2. Scenario 2, class imbalance: given a fraud dataset where a naive model hit 99.5% accuracy by never predicting fraud, I explained why accuracy fails here, compared class weighting against SMOTE oversampling, and identified precision, recall, F1, and precision-recall AUC as the metrics that actually matter for this problem.
  3. Scenario 3, algorithmic fairness: given a loan approval model where zip code was acting as a proxy for race, I asked a clarifying question about which fairness definition to target before answering, since demographic parity and equalized odds can conflict, then reasoned through why removing the proxy column alone is insufficient and proposed checking for proxies more broadly and applying a fairness-aware technique like adversarial debiasing.
  4. Throughout: I asked a follow-up or clarifying question in every scenario rather than treating the AI's first response as final, closing the activity by tying all three scenarios back to one underlying principle, understand why the data looks the way it does before choosing how to fix it.

Value Proposition
For hiring managers evaluating applied ML judgment: this artifact shows I can reason through ambiguous, real-world data problems under uncertainty, including the ethical and regulatory dimensions of model fairness, not just recite the correct terminology.
Unique Value

Unlike Artifact 3, which builds conceptual mastery of how models learn, this artifact demonstrates applied decision-making across a full data pipeline, from raw data quality problems through model evaluation to the fairness and compliance concerns that come with deploying a model on real people. The clarifying questions I asked before answering, especially in the fairness scenario, are a deliberate habit: confirming the actual goal before committing to a technical approach.

The Signal: AI in Business Newsletter

AIML-500 · Commercial AI Applications Newsletter

Where AI Is Actually Making Money Right Now

A subscriber-style newsletter analyzing how AI is already transforming business decisions and operations across three industries, software development, rail freight transportation, and banking, grounded in real, dated commercial deployments rather than speculative capability.

Description

The Signal is a newsletter-format analysis built around one constraint: every claim had to trace back to a specific, dated commercial deployment, not a demo or a roadmap slide. It covers three deliberately different industries, AI coding agents reshaping how engineering teams budget for tools, AI-driven predictive maintenance cutting derailments on freight rail, and agentic AI compressing bank fraud and anti-money-laundering investigations from days to minutes, and closes by naming the pattern connecting all three: AI moving from a tool that flags problems for a human to a system that takes the first pass at solving them.

Objective

I wanted to move past the generic "AI is transforming industry X" framing and show, with named companies, dates, and figures, what commercial AI adoption actually looks like right now, translated for a business audience that may not have deep technical background. The three industries were chosen deliberately: software development sits inside my own specialization, rail freight ties to my current role at a Class I railroad, and banking demonstrates I can research and write credibly outside either.

Process
  1. Scope and select: chose three industries with genuinely different relationships to AI (developer tooling, physical infrastructure safety, financial compliance) rather than three variations on the same theme, to demonstrate range rather than depth in one niche.
  2. Research and verify: researched recent, dated commercial developments in each industry, cross-checking company names, product launches, and figures against multiple sources before including them, and explicitly labeled illustrative estimates as illustrative rather than presenting them as precise statistics.
  3. Translate for the audience: rewrote every technical detail for a subscriber who may not know what "agentic AI" means, leading each section with the business impact rather than the underlying model architecture.
  4. Design for the format: built it as an actual newsletter, masthead, byline, section dividers, and a supporting chart, rather than a plain essay, since the assignment's audience is subscribers, not a professor grading a paper.
  5. Disclose AI use: included a dedicated AI Usage Disclosure section naming exactly where AI assistance was used (research verification, drafting help) and what stayed my own judgment (industry selection, analysis, framing).

Every other artifact in this portfolio shows what I can build or how I reason through technical problems.
